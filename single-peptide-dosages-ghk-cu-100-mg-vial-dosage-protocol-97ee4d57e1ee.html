GHK-Cu Dosage Chart

Dosing & Reconstitution Guide

Educational guide for reconstitution and common dosing patterns

Standard / Conservative Approach (3 mL = 33.33 mg/mL; 5 days/week)

Week/Phase Dose per Injection Units (per injection) (mL)
Weeks 1-4 1.0 mg (1000 mcg) 3 units (0.03 mL)
Weeks 5-8 1.5 mg (1500 mcg) 4.5 units (0.045 mL)
Weeks 9-12+ 2.0 mg (2000 mcg) 6 units (0.06 mL)

Frequency: Once daily, 5 days per week, progressing from 1.0 mg to 1.5 mg and then 2.0 mg as shown above. At 33.33 mg/mL, those doses equal 3, 4.5, and 6 U-100 units. Use a U-100 syringe with markings that clearly display every value in the table.

Reconstitution Steps

  1. Draw 3.0 mL of the protocol-specified sterile diluent with a sterile syringe.
  2. Add it slowly down the vial wall to reduce foaming.
  3. Gently swirl or roll until dissolved. Do not shake.
  4. Label the vial with the 33.33 mg/mL concentration, preparation date, and storage instructions.

Alternative Protocol (3 mL = 33.33 mg/mL; 3 times weekly)

Week/Phase Dose per Injection Units (per injection) (mL)
Weeks 1-12+ 2.0 mg (2000 mcg) 6 units (0.06 mL)

Frequency: 2.0 mg three times per week, such as Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. At 33.33 mg/mL, each injection equals 6 U-100 units (0.06 mL), for approximately 6 mg per week.

Important: This guide is for educational purposes only and is not medical advice. For research use only. Not for human consumption.

GHK-Cu Research Evidence by Route

GHK-Cu research is not interchangeable across topical, wound-care, local, and systemic routes. Published human studies have primarily examined topical creams, gels, serums, skin penetration, and wound-care formulations[1][2][3][4][5]. Local administration in an animal wound model adds preclinical context, but it does not validate a human subcutaneous GHK-Cu dose[6].

The tables on this page are therefore calculation examples for a 100 mg vial. No controlled human trial has established an approved dosing schedule for injected GHK-Cu, an injection frequency, a cycle length, or a systemic benefit. Route, formulation, concentration, and population must remain explicit whenever the evidence is interpreted.

How GHK-Cu Works in Laboratory Research

GHK-Cu is a naturally occurring copper peptide formed when the tripeptide glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine binds copper. Reviews describe GHK-Cu research involving extracellular-matrix signaling, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways, collagen-related activity, tissue repair, and gene-expression patterns[7]. Experimental work also reports GHK-related modulation of matrix metalloproteinase-2, an enzyme involved in matrix remodeling[8].

Research on GHK-Cu describes a network of biological effects rather than one switch. In laboratory research, GHK-Cu acts as a copper-binding complex; GHK-Cu signals have been studied in matrix regulation; and GHK-Cu influences gene-expression patterns. Reviews discuss how GHK-Cu supports tissue-remodeling research. The shorthand claim that GHK-Cu works by stimulating one isolated target is too narrow because multiple interacting processes are described[7][8]. Those mechanisms help explain why research on this copper peptide often focuses on skin structure, wound healing, collagen, and remodeling. Any claim about GHK-Cu benefits must still be matched to route, formulation, and study design. They do not by themselves establish that an injected GHK-Cu preparation works for a particular clinical outcome, nor do they determine a recommended dose.

10

GHK-Cu vs GHK: Why Copper Binding Matters

GHK is a peptide made from three amino acids: glycine, histidine, and lysine. GHK-Cu is the same peptide bound to a copper ion. In research terminology, GHK-Cu is a copper-bound complex and a copper-binding peptide; the phrase GHK-Cu copper peptide distinguishes the complex from unbound GHK.

The peptide was identified in human plasma, and more than 50 years of research have explored GHK-Cu gene expression, collagen synthesis, collagen production, extracellular-matrix activity, and tissue remodeling[7][8]. GHK-Cu is a copper peptide with broad laboratory interest, but mechanistic findings do not establish a clinical dose or prove that a specific administration route is effective.

100 mg GHK-Cu Vial Calculations

Preparing a 100 mg vial to 3.0 mL produces 33.33 mg/mL. On a U-100 syringe, one unit equals 0.01 mL, so one unit contains approximately 0.333 mg, or 333.3 mcg, of GHK-Cu.

  • 1.0 mg dose: 1.0 ÷ 33.33 = 0.03 mL = 3 U-100 units.
  • 1.5 mg dose: 1.5 ÷ 33.33 = 0.045 mL = 4.5 U-100 units.
  • 2.0 mg dose: 2.0 ÷ 33.33 = 0.06 mL = 6 U-100 units.

The larger vial changes concentration and supply planning; it does not make a higher GHK-Cu dose more appropriate. A 50mg GHK-Cu vial can require a different conversion, so the unit values from one vial setup should not be transferred to another. The syringe marking must be recalculated whenever vial mass or final volume changes.

How much GHK-Cu is in each unit? At this concentration, each unit contains about 0.333 mg. Dosage ranges found in online discussions do not replace route-specific evidence. The important dosage considerations are vial mass, final volume, concentration, measured dose, and frequency; peptide dosing arithmetic does not establish a recommended regimen.

Human GHK-Cu Skin and Wound Research

Human evidence includes a diabetic-ulcer study of a topical copper peptide formulation, a postoperative topical study after carbon-dioxide laser resurfacing, comparative cream research, a split-face GHK-Cu serum and copper peptide serum study, and human-skin penetration work[1][2][3][4][5]. Published human studies used GHK-Cu in topical copper peptide products. These studies are relevant to topical delivery and wound formulations, not to the subcutaneous schedule in the chart. The formulation studies measured outcomes related to skin quality and collagen and elastin; they do not establish improved skin from injections.

An ongoing Phase 2 study is evaluating topical GHK-Cu gel for acute skin-wound healing[9]. FDA records also include a topical Iamin Gel wound-dressing device, which is route- and product-specific and should not be interpreted as approval of a GHK-Cu injection[12].

Injectable GHK-Cu: Safety and Evidence Limits

No injectable formulation of GHK-Cu has an established FDA-approved human dosing regimen. FDA compounding materials identify potential safety concerns for injections containing GHK-Cu and note that available human safety information is limited[10]. FDA 503A materials also treat route as part of the substance evaluation, reinforcing that topical findings cannot be converted into a systemic injection schedule[11].

Possible risks can depend on sterility, endotoxin control, concentration, excipients, copper exposure, injection technique, and individual health factors. A calculation can show how much material is in a syringe; it cannot prove safety, efficacy, or suitability for human use.

The phrase GHK-Cu peptide therapy appears in consumer searches, but no approved GHK-Cu therapy or medically validated peptide protocol is established. GHK-Cu administration on this page is calculation context only. It does not instruct readers to combine GHK-Cu, build a GHK-Cu stack, or treat this page as a medically supervised peptide therapy guide. Claims that GHK-Cu has a strong safety profile for injection are not supported by robust injectable human data, and current evidence does not show that injected GHK-Cu accelerates healing in humans. A syringe calculation does not determine whether or how to inject GHK-Cu. Likewise, a GHK-Cu injection search result is not evidence of safety or effectiveness.

Injection Technique and Research Records

GHK-Cu Frequently Asked Questions

These frequently asked questions separate vial calculations from what the research can actually support.

What is GHK-Cu?

GHK-Cu is a copper-binding tripeptide, also called copper tripeptide-1. It is studied in skin, extracellular-matrix, collagen, gene-expression, and tissue-repair research[7][8].

What does a 100 mg GHK-Cu vial change?

Vial strength changes concentration, syringe units, and supply totals. With a 3.0 mL final volume, the concentration is 33.33 mg/mL. It does not change the evidence supporting a dose.

How many U-100 units equal 1 mg of GHK-Cu?

At 33.33 mg/mL, 1 mg equals 0.03 mL or 3 U-100 units. This conversion applies only to the 100 mg vial prepared to exactly 3.0 mL.

Is there a clinically established daily injection dose for GHK-Cu?

No controlled human trial has established a clinically approved daily dose for an injected GHK-Cu preparation. The chart displays community-reported use examples and arithmetic conversions, not medical recommendations.

Can topical research support a GHK-Cu injection schedule?

No. Topical skin and wound-healing studies provide formulation-specific evidence, but they do not validate subcutaneous dose, frequency, cycle length, or systemic outcomes[1][5].

Is there a standard GHK-Cu dosage?

No standard GHK-Cu dosage is established for injection. Common GHK-Cu dosing claims and dosage ranges should not be treated as validated clinical guidance.

What about GHK-Cu for hair?

Skin and hair searches often connect GHK-Cu with hair growth, hair loss, microneedling, and the phase of the hair cycle. GHK-Cu hair claims and cosmetic peptide claims require formulation-specific evidence. The sources cited on this page do not establish an injected dose for those outcomes, and this chart should not be read as a treatment protocol.

Is injectable GHK-Cu FDA approved?

No FDA-approved injection dosing regimen for GHK-Cu is established. FDA compounding materials discuss potential risks and limited human safety information for injection use of GHK-Cu[10][11].
